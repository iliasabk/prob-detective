Probability Detective — when does a pattern mean something?

A 10–15 minute lab for readers around 12. You will flip coins, roll dice and design a fair game — then argue from evidence, not gut feeling. Everything is simulated on your own device; nothing is real gambling and nothing is collected.

Briefing

A detective's rule: theory says what should happen; experiments show what did happen — and the two rarely match exactly in small samples.

  • Lab 1 — flip a coin 10, 100 and 1,000 times and watch the proportion of heads settle;
  • Lab 2 — roll two dice and discover why some totals are common and others rare;
  • Lab 3 — build a points game, test it, and judge whether both sides have an equal chance.
Inline definition: theoretical probability = what the model predicts (e.g. 1/2 for heads). Observed frequency = what your trials actually produced. They are cousins, not twins.

Takes about 10–15 minutes. Keyboard works everywhere (Tab + Enter). Your progress stays in this page only.

Lab 3 · Fair-game designer

Design a points game: You flip the mystery coin; your friend picks the rule. If heads, you score your points; if tails, your friend scores theirs. A fair game gives both sides the same expected advantage per flip.

Checkpoint. With the biased 70% coin, how could you make the game fair?

Final challenge — describe your own fair game

Invent a points rule that is fair even with a biased coin (e.g. "I get 3 on heads, friend gets 7 on tails, on the 70% coin"). Explain in one or two sentences why the expected advantage is equal.

Carry this forward

When someone claims a pattern, ask: How big was the sample? What does theory predict? Is the game rigged — or just random? Try tonight: flip a real coin 20 times, note the run, then ask whether 12 heads would surprise you.

About the simulations. Coins, dice and games are simulated in your browser with a seeded generator (see README/EDUCATOR_GUIDE). No real money, gambling or personal data is involved; all names and places are fictional.
